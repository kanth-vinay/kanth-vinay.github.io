More Isn't Better: The Profile Problem Every "Good on Paper" High Schooler Falls Into

A strong ACT score and a GPA above 3.8 used to be enough to stand out. According to the same admissions data colleges themselves report, that's no longer where the decision gets made, and most families never hear why.

~6 min read By Prof. Vinay Kanth Rao Kodipelly
View My Portfolio

Does a strong ACT score and a GPA above 3.8 still make an application stand out? For a growing number of families I talk with, the honest answer is no, and most of them are surprised to hear it, because nothing about the transcript changed. What changed is how many other applications look exactly the same.

What admissions officers actually weigh most

Grades in courses 75%
Curriculum rigor 64%
Application essay 24%
Extracurriculars 7%

Share of NACAC member colleges rating each factor "considerable importance," Fall 2023 cycle. Source: NACAC, State of College Admission Report, 2023.

According to that same NACAC survey, only seven percent of member colleges call extracurricular activities a factor of considerable importance on their own, next to seventy-five percent for grades and sixty-four percent for curriculum rigor. Read quickly, that looks like good news: the list barely matters. Read carefully, it says something sharper. When academics are strong across nearly every applicant in a given pool, and they usually are among the families I work with, the list is often what's left to tell two nearly identical transcripts apart. A thin sliver of weight, applied to a crowded field, still decides quite a lot.

The Profile, Almost Word for Word

Ask any experienced counselor to describe a strong, typical applicant from an academically engaged family, and you will hear close to the same paragraph every time.

A solid ACT or SAT score. A GPA comfortably above 3.8. Debate or a HOSA chapter, sometimes both, alongside a club or two. A few years of consistent volunteering, usually through a community organization. Some years of an instrument or a classical art form, kept up dutifully alongside schoolwork.

None of this is a complaint about the students. Every one of those activities takes real years of discipline to build, and I say that as someone who has coached students through most of them. The problem is not the effort. The problem is that when a reader has seen this exact combination hundreds of times from students with a similar background, the combination itself stops carrying information. It reads as a template, not a story.

Why Opportunity Changes the Read

This is the part families often miss, and it matters as much as the profile itself. Admissions offices do not read an application in isolation. They read it against what was realistically available to that student, in that school, in that community, because that is the only fair way to compare a student with limited access to advanced coursework against a student who had a university, a research lab, or a strong STEM ecosystem nearby. This is not an assumption on my part: it is the explicit purpose of the school profile document that every high school counselor sends alongside a student's transcript, and NACAC's own guidance describes it as the tool that lets an admissions reader "decode" a student's record against their school and community context.

That context cuts both ways. For a student with genuinely limited access to research mentors, internships, or advanced coursework, admissions readers calibrate for that, and rightly so. But for a student whose school, city, or family network already has real access to those things, the calibration runs the opposite direction. When a reader sees a strong but conventional profile coming out of an environment with real opportunity nearby, the natural question is not "what did this student achieve," it is "why didn't this student reach for what was sitting right in front of them."

That question is rarely written down anywhere. But it shapes the read. A profile that would look ambitious from a resource-limited setting can look like the convenient, lower-effort path when it comes from a setting with more on offer. It is not a judgment about the student's character. It is a judgment about fit between effort and opportunity, and that bar keeps rising as access keeps growing, no matter which part of the country a family calls home.

The Convenient Track, and Why It's Easy to Spot

I see this pattern often, at the scale of individual students. A student with access to a strong teacher, a research-adjacent program, or a genuine mentor nearby, who instead sticks to the safest, most familiar extracurricular menu, the one a counselor could have assembled from a checklist five years ago. It is not laziness in the way that word usually gets used. It is comfort. The activity is known, the community around it is known, the outcome is predictable. But predictability is exactly what a differentiated application cannot afford.

Where one more activity stops adding signal

Illustrative only, not measured data. The point: a second, third, or fourth version of a familiar activity adds progressively less to how an application reads, while depth in one self-directed project keeps compounding.

The students I have watched stand out are rarely the ones who did more activities. They are the ones who did fewer things, but chose the ones that were harder to find, harder to sustain, and closer to something they genuinely could not stop thinking about. In every case, the starting point was the same: a mentor helping a student notice an opportunity that was already nearby but invisible to them, followed by the student doing the outreach and the follow-through themselves.

A few examples from my own students make this concrete.

Same hours, two different shapes

  • Joins an existing club because a friend is already in it
  • Volunteers a set number of hours a year through a standing program
  • Keeps up an instrument at the same level for several years
  • Application entry: a title and a member-since date
  • Arranges shadowing with a practicing professional, on her own initiative
  • Turns that experience into an independent research submission
  • Designs and runs an outreach event teaching what she learned to younger students
  • Application entry: a specific contribution with a name and a result

One student I mentor arranged a month of clinical shadowing with a practicing cardiologist, learning to read EKGs and interpret angiograms, and used that experience as the seed for an independent research submission on cardiovascular health-seeking behavior. From there, she organized a library event where she brought in practicing doctors to teach cardiology basics to sixteen middle schoolers from the area, work she designed and ran herself, with me providing the research framework and feedback along the way. That submission has since been accepted for presentation at ICERI2026, the International Conference on Education, Research and Innovation.

A ninth grader I coach is co-authoring a statistics research project examining whether multiple-choice assessments actually measure reasoning or just recognition, a question that grew out of something he noticed in his own coursework and that we then built into a formal study design. That project has also been accepted for presentation at ICERI2026, and we are now preparing it for journal submission. Another student is working through an independent research paper alongside her regular coursework, not because a program assigned it to her, but because she chose to build it with guidance rather than a template. None of these came from a checklist. Each came from a student asking what was actually available, a mentor helping sharpen that question, and then the student doing the harder version of it instead of the convenient one.

What This Looks Like in Practice

A few things hold true regardless of city or school.

  • Depth Beats Breadth, Especially in a Saturated Profile. One sustained, specific body of work reads more clearly than five broad, familiar ones.
  • The Rarer Signal Is Usually the One That Took Longer to Build. A student-authored project, a genuine research contribution, or an unusually advanced course sequence says more than another year of the same club.
  • Opportunity Is Not Optional Context, It Is the Baseline a Reader Measures Against. What counts as reaching in one setting counts as expected in another.
  • Comfort Is the Quiet Enemy of Differentiation. The activities that feel safest to continue are often the ones doing the least work for the application.

Starting Earlier, in Middle School

None of this needs to begin in tenth or eleventh grade, and it is easier to build well before then. The habit that matters most in middle school is not a specific activity, it is the practice of noticing what is nearby and asking a genuine question about it, then following that question somewhere on paper before it becomes a formal project.

The habit worth starting in middle school

The same loop, repeated small, is what makes a self-directed project possible later. A student who spends middle school practicing it arrives in high school already knowing how to turn a stray interest into real work.

A student who spends middle school learning to notice, ask, and follow through on a small scale arrives in high school already knowing how to turn a stray interest into real work, instead of discovering that skill for the first time under application pressure.

What This Means for Your Family

If your family recognizes any part of that opening description, the point of this piece is not to create alarm, it is to make the pattern visible enough to act on. Depth reads. Repetition doesn't. The list itself was never the differentiator, and treating it as one is exactly what produces the convenient, checklist-shaped profile that blends into every other file in the pool. What actually separates an application is whether a student stayed with one real question long enough to produce something specific, whether that work started from genuine curiosity rather than a template, and whether the habit of noticing, asking, and following through was built early enough to feel natural under pressure rather than invented at the last minute. None of that requires more activities. It requires fewer, chosen more deliberately, and followed further than convenience would allow.
