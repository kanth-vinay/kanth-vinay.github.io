What Your Report Should Look Like

One PDF. No loose files. A professional report flows like this:

  • Introduction: One short paragraph. What data are you analyzing and why.
  • Methods and Results: One section per task (six total). State the method you chose and justify it, show your R code and output, check assumptions, and write a plain-English conclusion. Imagine explaining it to a friend who took Bio 101, not a statistician.
  • Discussion and Conclusion: Step back and summarize. What did you find? What are the limitations? What would you tell me, the person whose data this is, about my recovery habits?
Write for a real audience. Your final interpretation for every task should be something I could read without knowing what a p-value is and still understand what you found. If your conclusion says "we reject H₀ at α = 0.05" and nothing else, that's incomplete.

The Dataset

All 45 observations come from my Pixel Watch 3, recorded automatically each night from August 24 through October 7, 2026. No manual entry. Whatever the watch logged is what you get.

Variable Descriptions

  • SleepMinutes: Total minutes asleep that night
  • REMMinutes: Minutes spent in REM sleep
  • DeepSleepMinutes: Minutes spent in deep sleep
  • HRV: Overnight average heart-rate variability, in milliseconds
  • ReadinessScore: Daily recovery score (0 to 100) computed by the watch each morning
  • DayType: Weekday (Mon to Fri) or Weekend (Sat to Sun)
  • Phase: Phase1 (Aug 24 to Sep 7), Phase2 (Sep 8 to Sep 22), Phase3 (Sep 23 to Oct 7)
  • ReadyZone: Was ReadinessScore 72 or higher (the watch's "green zone")? Yes or No

A Quick Note on the Data

Keep that in mind when you interpret any pattern you find there. The Questions

  1. Am I actually well-rested by the numbers?

    Sleep guidelines commonly cite 420 minutes (7 hours) per night as the benchmark for adequate adult sleep. Using the method assigned to your last-name band, evaluate whether my average nightly sleep is consistent with this benchmark. Report a 95% CI for the true mean and interpret it in context. Do the data support the claim that I meet this threshold, or fall short?

  2. Does my HRV differ between weeknights and weekends?

    At first glance, you might expect weekends to show higher HRV, given less schedule pressure and more downtime. But weeknight routine and consistent bedtimes could just as easily produce steadier recovery. Using the method assigned to your band, evaluate whether a statistically significant difference in average HRV exists between weekdays and weekends. Provide a 95% CI for the difference and explain what that interval tells you in plain English.

  3. A bold claim about hitting the green zone.

    Someone claims I land in the ReadinessScore "green zone" on more than 65% of nights during this period. Before attempting any analysis, carefully check whether all required mathematical conditions for this type of claim are satisfied. Show your work. If any condition fails, state clearly which one and why, and explain what that means for your ability to draw conclusions from this data.

  4. Is landing in the green zone related to the day of the week?

    It seems intuitive that weekdays (a fuller schedule) might produce different readiness outcomes than weekends (more rest). But is that actually supported by the data? Determine whether a statistically significant relationship exists between DayType and ReadyZone. Report all relevant statistics, including a two-way frequency summary with both observed and expected values, and interpret your findings. Address any conditions that may affect the reliability of your conclusion.

  5. Did my recovery change across the three phases of the semester?

    The dataset is split into three 15-day phases: Phase1 (Aug 24 to Sep 7), Phase2 (Sep 8 to Sep 22), and Phase3 (Sep 23 to Oct 7). Determine whether the average HRV is the same across all three phases or whether at least one phase stands out. Report the complete test output, a 95% CI for each group mean, and include side-by-side boxplots. If results are significant, discuss which phases appear to differ and what that might mean, and remember to consider what else was happening during that window before you draw a conclusion.

  6. Can HRV help predict my Readiness Score?

    The Pixel Watch 3 computes HRV and ReadinessScore independently each morning, but they should be related. Determine whether HRV can serve as a useful predictor of ReadinessScore. Report the fitted equation, the proportion of variability explained, a 95% CI for the rate of change, and a scatterplot with the fitted line overlaid. Interpret the rate of change in plain English: if my HRV is 5 ms higher tomorrow morning, how much higher should I expect my Readiness Score to be?
