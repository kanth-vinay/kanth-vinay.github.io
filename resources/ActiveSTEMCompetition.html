Q5

Squaring the Storm

Ahead of a predicted storm system on June 13, 2026, two meteorologists are mapping the affected region for St. Louis area residents — a zone stretching from Wentzville all the way to the Illinois border, a span of 50 miles.

One meteorologist proposes warning everyone inside a circle with that 50-mile span as its diameter. The other proposes warning everyone inside a square with that same 50-mile span as its side length, so that the circle sits exactly inside the square, touching all four sides.

Storm zone proposals — not to scale
PART A
If officials use the circle plan, some residents inside the square zone would never receive a warning — the corner regions covered by the square but missed by the circle. Find the exact area of this trapped region. Leave $\pi$ in your answer; do not convert it to a decimal.
PART B
Suppose officials instead wanted a square warning zone with area exactly equal to the circle's area — no missed residents, no wasted coverage. What side length would that square need? Give your answer in exact form.
PART C — THINK ABOUT IT
You just calculated that side length using algebra. But here is something remarkable: no mapmaker, using only a compass and a straightedge, could ever draw that exact square — no matter how skilled or careful. This is because $\pi$ is a transcendental number, meaning it cannot be built from any finite sequence of square-root constructions, which is all a compass and straightedge can ever produce. Ancient Greek mathematicians wondered about this exact question — known as "squaring the circle" — for over 2,000 years before it was finally proven impossible in 1882. In your own words, explain why being able to calculate a length is not the same as being able to construct it.
