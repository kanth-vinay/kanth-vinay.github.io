Paid to Publish: What Parents Are Actually Buying

A documented industry now sells guaranteed research publications, and sometimes guaranteed authorship on work a student never touched, to families anxious about college admissions. Here is what the investigations actually found, and what a family is responsible for asking before paying.

~8 min read By Prof. Vinay Kanth Rao Kodipelly
View My Portfolio

In the summer of 2026, a parent in Saint Louis reached out and asked me, for lack of a better word, to add his son's name to a paper that was about to be published. He offered two thousand dollars, outright, for the addition alone. No data collected, no writing done, no involvement in the project at any stage. Just the name, and the fee. I was shocked, and that is an understatement.

What unsettled me more than the request itself was how casually it was made, as though this were a normal transaction rather than a fabricated authorship claim. It wasn't an isolated ask. Once I started paying attention, I found this is not a fringe practice. It is a documented, named industry.

Two Different Things, Often Confused

Before going further, these need to be separated, because they are not the same problem. Paying for structured mentorship, where a real mentor guides a student through real work and a publication is a possible outcome rather than a guarantee, is a legitimate and common practice. Paying to have a student's name added to work they did not do, or paying for a guaranteed publication regardless of the work's actual merit, is a different thing entirely. Payment itself is not the issue. The issue is when payment substitutes for contribution, or when an outcome that depends on independent peer review is sold as though it were a predictable, purchasable product.

Buying a Name, Not a Contribution

The request I received this past summer is the clearest version of this problem. A paper is already written, already near acceptance, sometimes already accepted. A parent, or a broker acting on a parent's behalf, offers to add a student's name to the author list, for a fee, with no actual work required.

If a student's name appears on a paper they did not meaningfully contribute to, that is a fabricated authorship claim. If someone later asks that student to explain the research question, the methodology, the analysis, or the revisions made along the way, a student whose contribution was largely nominal may have real difficulty accounting for the work carrying their name.

A Documented Industry, With Real Numbers

A 2023 investigation by ProPublica and the Chronicle of Higher Education examined this industry directly and found at least twenty such programs operating globally, serving roughly twelve thousand students a year, with families typically spending between $2,500 and $10,000. Individual company figures cited in the investigation include Scholar Launch, at $3,500 to $8,800 per program, and Athena Education, at over $12,200 annually. Lumiere Education has served more than 1,500 students since 2020, paying its mentors $150 to $200 an hour.

The same investigation found that one commonly cited outlet, the Journal of Student Research, accepted roughly 65 percent of submissions referred to it through certain paid placement services, despite marketing itself as a rigorously peer-reviewed journal. Reporters also found cases of mentor credentials being inflated, PhD students described to families as "PhDs," and journal editors reviewing papers submitted by their own paying mentees, a direct conflict of interest.

~65% of submissions referred through certain paid placement services were accepted by the Journal of Student Research, a rate reporters found notable given the journal's own claims of rigorous peer review. Genuine peer review is selective by design; a consistently high acceptance rate for a paying referral channel is itself a signal worth questioning.

What Admissions Officers Actually Said

The same investigation quoted an Ivy League admissions officer describing the trend plainly as a "fast-growing epidemic," and noting the obvious problem with evaluating it: "A sophomore in high school is not going to be doing high-level neuroscience." Another admissions reader acknowledged spending as little as ten minutes per application, without the scientific background to actually evaluate a submitted paper's legitimacy.

Kent Anderson, formerly of the New England Journal of Medicine, put the deeper cost plainly: the practice teaches students to be cynical about research itself, to learn that publication is something that "can be gotten," rather than something that is earned. MIT's dean of admissions, Stuart Schmill, separately acknowledged that access to real research opportunities isn't distributed equitably in the first place, which is part of why this market exists at all, even as his own institution continues to weigh these credentials on applications.

What Is Actually Being Bought

Beyond any single application cycle, there is a broader concern. A student who is handed a research credential, rather than one who builds it, is being taught something about how competence works, that it can be acquired rather than developed. For a student who has also spent years being rewarded for correctly filling out a worksheet rather than for genuine understanding, a purchased publication credit can reinforce the same idea in a more consequential form: that the appearance of achievement is interchangeable with achievement itself.

The Value in No Guarantee

Structured mentorship can be valuable when the student is responsible for the actual intellectual work, the mentor provides substantive guidance rather than a finished product, authorship reflects real contribution, and publication is treated as a possible outcome rather than a purchased deliverable. Under that model, there is no guarantee, because no honest mentor can guarantee what an independent reviewer will decide. A student who spends months on a project and sees it rejected, revised, or delayed has learned something a purchased credential cannot teach: that real work does not move on a marketing calendar.

What Parents Are Responsible For

Before paying for any research or publication program, or agreeing to add a child's name to a paper, a parent can reasonably ask: is a publication or placement being guaranteed in advance? Is the student actually doing the work behind the name being added? Does the journal have a real editorial board, a track record, and independently verifiable indexing, rather than existing mainly to publish students who pay to submit? Can the mentor speak concretely and specifically about the research itself, not just their availability?

Conclusion: A Credential Is Not a Contribution

The more useful question for a family to ask is not how quickly a child can be published, but what the child will actually understand by the end of the process that they didn't understand at the start. One of those questions can be answered with a fee and a fixed calendar. The other cannot be bought at any price, and that is exactly why it's worth something.
